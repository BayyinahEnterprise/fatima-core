Inheritance, Transmission, and Turning Back
A Reading of Formed Agency in the Qur’an and Prophetic Tradition
Bilal Syed Arfeen
July 23, 2026

Abstract
This paper concerns both kinds of creature the Qur’an holds responsible: mankind and the concealed ones (jinn). Both
are addressed together as recipients of messengers sent from among themselves (6:130), both are described as morally
various rather than uniformly corrupt, and both are described in terms that admit generations, communities, and
transmission within them. What follows is therefore not a human account later extended, and the section on both
responsible species sets out the sourcing.
A person may be formed, from infancy, into a tradition he did not choose, by people whose own formation he also did
not choose. His capacity to evaluate what he was given arrives only after the giving is complete, and the faculties he
would evaluate it with are among the things it shaped. This paper asks what the Qur’an and the prophetic tradition say
about his responsibility, and about the responsibility of those who formed him.
The answer developed here has three levels rather than two. Inherited position carries no culpability: the pen does not
record against a child, and the tradition frames corruption of the innate disposition as an injury done to him rather than
an act performed by him. Sustained participation after the arrival of capacity is where responsibility attaches, under
the condition that a conclusive proof has reached the person. Transmission, the act of forming the next, is where
weight attaches and where it compounds with the number who follow.
The paper then argues three consequences. Responsibility in the collaboration between mankind and the concealed
ones is not divided into shares. Each party answers in full for his own act, and neither one's weight is subtracted from
the other's. The sources describe arrangements freely sought for benefit, in which the human parties concede mutual
advantage in their own words; they supply a category for genuine compulsion; and they state a visibility asymmetry
that runs one way only. These bear on how each party is weighed rather than on how a quantity is apportioned
between them, and the weighing belongs to God. What does not vary across those conditions is transmission, where
the labour that formation requires is described nowhere as belonging to the concealed ones. What does not vary across
those conditions is transmission, where the labour that formation requires is described nowhere as belonging to the
concealed ones. The chain of formation terminates ontologically but not epistemically, and the tradition accordingly
resolves individual cases by the availability of a rebuke rather than by lineage. And the remedy, turning back, is
indexed to the person’s present position rather than to reconstruction of his history, which is why it remains available
to someone who cannot trace how he arrived.

The question
Consider a child born into a household that practices something the wider community holds to be corrupt. He is
inducted from infancy. The practices are presented to him as ordinary, and the people presenting them are the people
who feed him. Refusal would carry costs he has no capacity to bear, and in any case no vantage exists from which
refusal would occur to him. By the time he can weigh the tradition, it has weighed him. The household did not invent
the practice. His parents were formed as he is being formed, and theirs before them.

On both responsible species
The Qur’an addresses responsibility to two created kinds together, and does so in a single vocative: “O company of
jinn and mankind, did there not come to you messengers from among yourselves?” (ya ma’shar al-jinni wa al-insi
alam ya’tikum rusulun minkum, 6:130). Both are named as created for the same purpose: “I did not create jinn and
mankind except to worship Me” (wa ma khalaqtu al-jinna wa al-insa illa li-ya’buduni, 51:56). Two claims must be
kept apart here, and an earlier version of this paper ran them together.
That the concealed ones received messengers is not in dispute. They are addressed in the vocative alongside mankind
and asked whether messengers came; a company of them heard the recitation and returned to their people (46:29-32,
72:1-2); and the mission of the Prophet is to both kinds.
The concealed ones are described as possessing everything the structure of this paper requires.They have offspring.
Iblis is identified as one of them, and the verse asks whether you would take “him and his offspring” as allies besides
God (afa tattakhidhunahu wa dhurriyyatahu awliya’a min duni, 18:50). The word is the ordinary one for progeny.
They have a community. A group of them who heard the recitation “returned to their people as warners” (wallaw ila
qawmihim mundhirin, 46:29-32). They have moral variety, stated in their own reported speech rather than described
from outside: “among us are the righteous and among us are others than that; we are of differing ways” (wa anna
minna as-salihuna wa minna duna dhalika kunna tara’iqa qidada, 72:11), and “among us are those who submit and
among us are the unjust” (wa anna minna al-muslimuna wa minna al-qasitun, 72:14). And they have members who
mislead the rest: “our foolish one used to say about God an excess” (wa annahu kana yaqulu safihuna ’ala Allahi
shatata, 72:4). A community, generations within it, transmission across them, moral variety rather than uniform
corruption, and messengers arriving from among themselves.
Three questions separate here, and collapsing them produces the errors this paper is organised against.
First: is he culpable for having been placed there?
Second: what is required of him once capacity arrives?
Third: what happens when he, in turn, forms a child?
Ordinary intuition answers the first quickly. Nobody supposes an infant chose. What is less obvious is that answering
the first does not answer the second, and that the third question is where the tradition places most of the weight.

Level one: the pen is lifted
Two sources settle the first question directly.
The first is the prophetic ruling that “the pen is lifted from three” (rufi’a al-qalam ’an thalathah), the child until
maturity (bulugh) among them. Recorded at Abu Dawud 4398, graded sahih by al-Albani; at Tirmidhi 1423, graded
hasan; and at Nasa’i 3432, with al-Hakim grading it sahih upon the conditions of Muslim and Ibn Hajar noting in Fath
al-Bari that its paths strengthen one another. What the lifted pen removes is the recording of sins, not of good deeds.
The exemption is not leniency applied to a lesser offence. It is the absence of the recording relation itself. Where no
pen writes, there is no entry to weigh. Every child is born upon the innate natural disposition (fitrah), and then “his
parents make him a Jew, a Christian, or a Zoroastrian” (fa abawahu yuhawwidanihi aw yunassiranihi aw
yumajjisanihi).
Two features of this report deserve attention and are usually passed over. The verb takes the parents as its grammatical
subject. The action is theirs. The child is its object, not its agent. The sentence does not say the child becomes; it says
they make him. The report completes with a comparison to an animal born whole, asking whether you find it
mutilated. Corruption of the innate disposition is thereby framed as an injury inflicted, not a choice exercised. The
image is surgical, not judicial.
The Qur’an supplies the same disposition as the created default rather than as an absence: “the natural disposition of
God upon which He created mankind” (fitrat Allahi allati fatara an-nasa ’alayha, 30:30). This matters for the injury
model, though the reading is contested: classical interpretations of what the innate disposition denotes include Islam
specifically, the primordial covenant, natural recognition of the Creator, and a predisposition or receptivity rather than
a content. A default with content behaves differently under formation than an empty vessel would: something is
displaced rather than merely filled. And inherited guilt is blocked without qualification: “no bearer of burdens shall
bear the burden of another” (alla taziru waziratun wizra ukhra, 53:38), a formula the Qur’an repeats in several places,
which is itself an indication of how much weight it is meant to carry. The result: placement in a corrupt formation is a
location, not a moral event. It is a fact about where the person stands, not a thing he did.

Level two: when responsibility attaches
The two conditions
Responsibility (taklif) attaches at maturity (bulugh) together with rational capacity (’aql). Neither alone suffices,
which is why the pen remains lifted from one who has lost the second whatever his age.

The third condition, which is external
A further condition does not concern the person’s development at all: “We do not punish until We have sent a
messenger” (wa ma kunna mu’adhdhibina hatta nab’atha rasula, 17:15).
This is a striking allocation. The person’s own maturation is insufficient. Something must arrive from outside him.
The tradition names what must arrive the conclusive proof (hujjah), and the condition means that responsibility is not
purely a function of the agent’s internal state. It depends on what reached him.

Those who lived in an interval without a messenger (ahl al-fatrah) are treated as a distinct category on exactly this
basis. The category must be kept to individuals genuinely unreached. Extended into a general theory of the human
condition, it dissolves responsibility for most people and takes the entire structure down with it, including the parts
that place weight on those who form others. Appendix B records a predecessor document by this author that made
precisely that extension and what it cost.

The hard case, posed and not resolved
What constitutes the conclusive proof reaching a person formed inside a closed tradition?
The difficulty is not that no proof arrives. It is that his formation may have taught him what to do with proofs of that
shape. If the tradition that formed him supplies an account of why such challenges appear and how to dismiss them,
then the proof arrives and is disposed of by a faculty the formation installed for the purpose.
The Qur’anic material suggests the threshold is lower than that difficulty implies. Abraham, examined in the section
on lineage, needed no argument he could not have constructed himself. He needed an object his father had carved and
a question about it.

Level three: transmission, and weight that
compounds
Two sources place weight specifically on the act of forming another.
The first: “that they may bear their burdens in full on the Day of Resurrection, and some of the burdens of those they
misled without knowledge” (li-yahmilu awzarahum kamilatan yawma al-qiyamati wa min awzari alladhina
yudilluahum bi-ghayri ’ilm, 16:25).
The second is the report on instituted practice: whoever institutes a good practice has its reward and the reward of all
who act upon it after him, and whoever institutes an evil practice bears its burden (wizr) and the burden of all who act
upon it after him.

The apparent contradiction, and its resolution
These appear to contradict the blocking of inherited guilt at 53:38. If no bearer bears another’s burden, how does a
transmitter bear the burdens of those who followed him?
The resolution is that the transmitter’s added weight is his own act of transmitting, not another’s burden transferred
onto him.
This is not an isolated inference, Ibn Kathir states explicitly, commenting on 17:15, that there is no contradiction
between the no-bearer verses and 16:25: the caller to misguidance bears his own sin together with a share equal to his
followers’, without detracting the least from theirs. His comment on 16:25 makes the same point through the report
that whoever calls to misguidance bears a burden like those who follow him. The originator framing is standard, and
is often illustrated by the report concerning the first son of Adam, who bears a share of every subsequent killing
because he answers for his own originating act rather than because guilt is transferred to him. What he answers for is
what he did: he instituted. The number who followed is the measure of that act’s extent, not a quantity of other
people’s guilt migrating to his account. The formula at 53:38 blocks transfer. The instituted-practice report measures
magnitude.

The partnership: what each party answers for
The Qur’an describes a collaboration between mankind and the concealed ones (jinn) that is worth examining,
because it is a worked case of joint corruption with an explicit allocation of blame.

The mechanism named
“And thus We made for every prophet an enemy: satans among mankind and among the concealed ones, inspiring one
another with ornamented speech in delusion” (shayatina al-insi wa al-jinni yuhi ba’duhum ila ba’din zukhrufa al-qawli
ghurura, 6:112).
Both species are named as sources of the enmity, symmetrically. And the mechanism specified is ornamented
speech (zukhruf al-qawl), mutually exchanged, producing delusion (ghurur). Not compulsion, not possession, not
sorcery. Speech, amplified between two kinds of agent.
At the reckoning the collaboration is described from the human side without evasion. Told that they took much from
mankind, the answer given is “our Lord, we benefited from one another” (rabbana istamta’a ba’duna bi-ba’din,
6:128). Mutual benefit, freely conceded, on the day when concession costs everything.
And the party who initiated is the party who bears the cost: “men among mankind used to seek refuge with men
among the concealed ones, so they increased them only in burden” (fa zaduhum rahaqa, 72:6). The human sought the
arrangement. The human was the one made heavier by it.

The limit consistently placed on the tempter
“Indeed the plot of Satan has ever been weak” (inna kayda ash-shaytani kana da’ifa, 4:76). The operative noun is a
scheme or stratagem (kayd). To call a stratagem weak is to say something about what it can oblige, not about how
ingenious it is.
His authority extends only over those who take him as ally and patron (wali), 16:99-100.
And at the end, the disavowal: “I had no authority over you except that I called you and you responded to me, so do
not blame me but blame yourselves” (ma kana liya ’alaykum min sultanin illa an da’awtukum fastajabtum li, fa la
talumuni wa lumu anfusakum, 14:22), followed by “I cannot help you, nor can you help me” (ma ana bi-musrikhikum
wa ma antum bi-musrikhiyya).

The term rendered authority (sultan) is doing precise work here. What is denied is standing to compel, not capacity to
speak. He called; that is conceded. He could not oblige; that is the claim. The same structure recurs at 59:16, where he
says disbelieve and, once the man has, says “I am free of you” (inni bari’un minka).

On fear as a motive, and what the sources do with it
The account above treats such an arrangement as sought for benefit. A reader may reasonably object that a party might
enter one not for gain but from fear: fear of what refusing would cost him, or cost his household. The sources engage
this directly, and the engagement changes less of the structure than one might expect while adding something the
account was missing.
The sources acknowledge fear as a motive. The verse already cited describes men who sought refuge with men among
the concealed ones (ya’udhuna, 72:6). The verb is one of taking shelter, and shelter is sought from something. On the
ordinary understanding of the verse’s setting, the practice concerned protection from feared harm rather than
acquisition of advantage. So fear is not a reading imposed on the material; it is in the vocabulary of the verse that
grounds the whole discussion.
The sources acknowledge that harm is possible. They are not squeamish about it. The material on what was taught
describes separation between a man and his wife (2:102). Elsewhere a condition is described as arising from a touch
(2:275). And a prophet reports affliction of exactly this kind (38:41) without any suggestion that he was thereby
brought to wrongdoing, which is the distinction the next paragraph turns on. The sources bound the harm in the same
breath. The verse describing that separation continues: “and they harm no one thereby except by God’s
permission” (wa ma hum bi-darrina bihi min ahadin illa bi-idhni Allah, 2:102). Whatever capacity is being described,
it is not autonomous.
What the sources deny is specifically compulsion. “Over My servants you have no authority, except those of the
deviators who follow you” (inna ’ibadi laysa laka ’alayhim sultanun illa mani ittaba’aka mina al-ghawin, 15:42), and
again “over My servants you have no authority” (17:65), alongside 16:99-100 and the weakness of the stratagem at
4:76. Affliction and compulsion are different claims, and the texts grant the first while denying the second. A person
may be harmed. He cannot be made to consent.
The tradition nonetheless has a category for genuine duress, and it should be named rather than left for a reader to
wonder about. Of one who utters disbelief under compulsion, the exception is explicit: “except one who is compelled
while his heart is at rest in faith” (illa man ukriha wa qalbuhu mutma’innun bi’l-iman, 16:106). Coercion is not treated
as though it were free choice.
The tradition names the refuge it prescribes, which is the part most directly answering the objection. Where a
disturbance comes, the instruction is to seek refuge with God (41:36, 7:200). The two closing chapters of the Qur’an
are given over entirely to this, and the last of them names the source of the whispering as “from among the jinn and
mankind” (mina al-jinnati wa’n-nas, 114:6), which is to say that the refuge prescribed covers precisely the harm under
discussion, from either kind. Read against 72:6, the structure is plain: refuge was sought, and it was sought in the
wrong place, and the seekers were increased only in burden.
Three consequences for this paper’s structure.
Fear may explain an entry without excusing a transmission. Even granting duress at the point of entry, and granting
the category at 16:106 its full force, what the frightened party subsequently does to those who come after him is a
separate act with its own account. the section on level three attaches weight to transmission, and fear at the moment of
entry is not a fact about the raising of a child two decades later.
Ancestral duress is not itself inherited, though present pressure is a separate question. A descendant inherits a practice;
he does not inherit his ancestor’s threat, and it is not a thing he can draw on merely by descent. A descendant may face

present threats of his own, may stand inside coercive institutions, and may live under continuing fear, and where he
does the category at 16:106 is his to invoke in his own right. What he cannot do is invoke it by inheritance.
The refusal case is exactly what the compulsion verses address. A person who declines such an arrangement and relies
on God is the person those verses name.

What persecution is, and what it is not evidence of
That believers are persecuted is common, attested, and central to the Qur’anic narrative. It is not a marginal possibility
to be hedged. “You will surely be tested in your wealth and yourselves, and you will surely hear much abuse from
those given the Book before you and from those who associate partners with God” (3:186). “Do people think they will
be left to say we believe and not be tested?” (29:2). “Or do you suppose you will enter the Garden without there
having come to you the like of what came to those who passed before you? Hardship and adversity touched them, and
they were shaken” (2:214). Of the People of the Ditch: “they resented them for nothing except that they believed in
God” (85:8). And the first permission to fight is granted on exactly this ground, “because they were wronged” (22:39).
The prophets are persecuted, their followers are persecuted, and the pattern is stated as expectation rather than as
accident. What remains unestablished is something narrower and different: whether declining an inherited
arrangement of the kind described above in fact produces retaliation from the concealed ones, as a general causal
matter.
The tradition closes this directly, in the same passage that promises the testing. The verse that asks whether people
expect to be left untested continues: “and God will surely make evident those who are truthful, and He will surely
make evident the liars” (29:3). The trial distinguishes; it does not certify. Both the truthful and the liars pass through
it, which is precisely why passing through it establishes nothing about which one is passing through. A reader who is
genuinely persecuted for his faith stands where many before him have stood, and the tradition tells him what that is
and what it is not: a trial he shares with the prophets, and not a receipt.

The Partnership: Joint corruption, undivided responsibility
An objection stands against any account that distributes responsibility evenly here, and the Qur’an states its basis
more plainly than the objection usually does.
The verse cited above for its tribal term says something else first. It comes inside a warning to the children of Adam
not to be afflicted as their parents were driven from the Garden, and it gives the reason: “he sees you, he and his tribe,
from where you do not see them” (innahu yarakum huwa wa qabiluhu min haythu la tarawnahum, 7:27). The
asymmetry of sight is named by the text, in a warning, as the hazard the warning concerns.
Three things follow, and they do not all run the same way.
The asymmetry is real, and it bears on consent. A party who cannot observe the other cannot assess what is being
offered, who is offering it, or what is intended by the offer. In ordinary moral reasoning an information asymmetry
between parties to an agreement is not a neutral fact about the agreement, and there is no reason to treat it as neutral
here.
The asymmetry is disclosed, which is a different situation from an undisclosed one. The verse is a warning, and a
warning presupposes that the warned party can act on it. Mankind is not described as labouring under a disadvantage
it has not been told about. It is told, in the text, that there is an unseen party that sees, that this party has a stated
purpose, and that caution is therefore required. Disclosure does not equalise the position. It converts an unwitting
disadvantage into a known one, and the tradition’s response to a known one is the refuge named in the preceding
subsection rather than the arrangement.
The tradition still assigns the human a share, in the human’s own voice. At the reckoning the human parties do not
plead the asymmetry. They say “our Lord, we benefited from one another” (6:128), which is a concession offered on

the day when concession costs everything and could as easily have been a complaint. The other party’s closing speech
is an instruction to blame yourselves (14:22). Whatever weight the asymmetry carries, the sources do not present it as
relieving the human party in the cases they describe.
Responsibility here is not divided into shares. Each party answers in full for his own act, and neither one's weight is
subtracted from the other's. The commentary on 16:25 is explicit on this: the caller bears his own sin together with a
share equal to his followers', without detracting the least from theirs. The conditions the texts name, an arrangement
sought for advantage, genuine compulsion, a visibility asymmetry running one way, bear on how each party is
weighed rather than on how a quantity is apportioned between them. And the weighing is not ours to perform. What
any share amounts to is measured by God, who does not wrong so much as an atom's weight (4:40), and for each are
degrees according to what they did (6:132).

The consequence
A caller with no standing to compel cannot raise a child. It cannot feed him, name him, teach him what is real, arrange
the social cost of his refusal, or be present across the years that formation requires. Every element of a transmission
that persists across generations requires sustained human labour. Strip the human contribution and the chain ends in a
single generation.

A worked instance: an inherited arrangement
sought for worldly benefit
The case. A household seeks benefit through an arrangement with the concealed ones (jinn). The arrangement is
understood by its participants as advantageous: protection, knowledge, standing, provision. It is transmitted to
children who did not seek it and who receive it as ordinary. Those children transmit it in turn.

The arrangement is human-initiated
The Qur’an is consistent that the seeking runs from the human side.

“Men among mankind used to seek refuge with men among the concealed ones, so they increased them only in
burden” (wa annahu kana rijalun mina al-insi ya’udhuna bi-rijalin mina al-jinni fa zaduhum rahaqa, 72:6). The verb is
one of seeking refuge or protection. The humans went looking.
And at the reckoning, the human parties are named by the relation itself: “their allies among mankind” (awliya’uhum
mina al-ins, 6:128), who answer “our Lord, we benefited from one another” (rabbana istamta’a ba’duna bi-ba’din).
The word rendered ally (wali) is the same term that appears at 16:99-100 as the sole basis of the tempter’s authority.
The relation is voluntary in both directions, and the benefit is conceded to have been mutual.

The motive is worldly, and the sources treat the gain as illusory
The transactional character is stated plainly in the material on what the satans taught: “they learn what harms them
and does not benefit them” (wa yata’allamuna ma yadurruhum wa la yanfa’uhum, 2:102), and of whoever purchased it
(ishtarahu), that he has no share in the hereafter.
This matters for how the case should be described. The tradition does not present the arrangement as an effective
bargain carrying a spiritual cost. It presents it as a bad transaction on its own terms: the purchaser acquires what harms
him, and the party who sought refuge is increased in burden (rahaq) rather than relieved of it. A treatment that warned
readers away from something it conceded worked would be describing a different thing than the sources describe.

Why such an arrangement persists across generations
Two features of the Qur’anic account bear on persistence, and both are about how the situation looks from inside it.
The first is beautification. “And We assigned to them companions who made attractive to them what was before them
and behind them” (wa qayyadna lahum qurana’a fa zayyanu lahum ma bayna aydihim wa ma khalfahum, 41:25). The
mechanism named is making a thing appear good, not compelling it. What is inherited is not only a practice but an
appearance under which the practice looks reasonable.
The second is the illusion of guidance. Of those who turn from the remembrance, “We assign to him a devil who
becomes his companion” (nuqayyid lahu shaytanan fa huwa lahu qarin, 43:36), and of such companions: “they bar
them from the way while they suppose themselves guided” (wa innahum la-yasudduunahum ’ani as-sabili wa
yahsabuna annahum muhtadun, 43:37).
The inheritor of such an arrangement is not described as knowingly wicked. He is described as mistaken about his
own standing.

The defence the inheritor makes, and its rejection
The Qur’an records the characteristic answer of a person defending an inherited practice, and records it in the
inheritor’s own voice: “We found our fathers upon a way, and we are following in their footsteps” (inna wajadna
aba’ana ’ala ummatin wa inna ’ala atharihim muhtadun, 43:22). The formula recurs at 2:170, 5:104, 7:28, and 31:21,
and 43:23 states that every warner before was met with the same answer by the affluent of his people.
Two things are notable. The defence is an appeal to lineage, which is the exact move the section on lineage shows the
Qur’an refusing. And the inheritor’s own word for his condition, in 43:22, is guided (muhtadun), the same word used
at 43:37 for those who suppose themselves guided while being barred. The inheritor is not lying about his belief. He is
wrong about it, and the wrongness has the shape the section on the guard describes.

What is claimed here
Claimed. That the sources describe arrangements between humans and the concealed ones as motivated by expected
benefit, treated as harmful to the one who sought them, sustained by a mechanism of beautification, accompanied by a
mistaken belief in one’s own guidance, and capable of being defended by appeal to ancestral practice. Each element is
cited above.
On the assigned companion, the universal assignment is established, not unknown: the prophetic report at Sahih
Muslim 2814a states that every person has a companion from among the concealed ones (qarin), and the Prophet’s
own was made to submit. The universally assigned companion may be the same appointment as the punitive
companion of 43:36, which is assigned as a consequence of turning away.

The parallel instance, on the other side
The case. A lineage or grouping among the concealed ones holds that its identity and purpose is the misleading of
mankind, and hands that understanding on to those who come after it.
What the sources establish.
A declared purpose, stated by its originator in the first person and at length. “Because You have put me in error, I will
surely sit in wait for them on Your straight path, then I will come to them from before them and from behind them and
from their right and from their left” (7:16-17). “I will surely make things attractive to them on the earth and mislead
them all” (la-uzayyinanna lahum fi’l-ardi wa la-ughwiyannahum ajma’in, 15:39). And of the human line

specifically, “I will surely take hold of his offspring, all but a few” (la-ahtanikanna dhurriyyatahu illa qalila, 17:62),
followed by an instruction to share with them in wealth and children (17:64).
A grouping, in the tribal term. “He sees you, he and his tribe, from where you do not see them” (innahu yarakum huwa
wa qabiluhu min haythu la tarawnahum, 7:27). The word rendered tribe (qabil) is a term of kind or clan.
Offspring. “Will you then take him and his offspring as allies besides Me, when they are enemies to you?” (afa
tattakhidhunahu wa dhurriyyatahu awliya’a min duni wa hum lakum ’aduww, 18:50). The same verse identifies him
as one of the concealed ones.
Peoples, differing ways, and members who mislead their own. Established at 46:29-32, 72:11, and 72:4, as set out in
the section on both responsible species.
What the sources do not establish, and this is the load-bearing gap. They give a founder’s declaration, a tribe, and
offspring. They do not state that the declared purpose is transmitted to that offspring as an inherited identity, in the
way a language or a household practice is transmitted, rather than adopted individually by each who adopts it. The
step from “he has offspring and a tribe” to “his offspring inherit his purpose as a tradition” is this paper’s inference
and not the text’s statement.
If the purpose is transmitted as an inheritance, then a member of that line stands at level one with respect to having
received it, and by the section on level one owes nothing for the receiving. If it is adopted individually, he stands at
level two, and everything the section on turning back says about turning back is available to him. There is no third
possibility in which he is condemned by his descent, because the section on lineage establishes that the tradition
refuses to resolve by lineage in either direction, and it refuses this for both kinds: the concealed ones testify about
themselves that they are of differing ways and that among them are those who submit (72:11, 72:14).
The founding declaration carries an exception that its own speaker states: “except Your chosen servants among
them” (illa ’ibadaka minhum al-mukhlasin, 15:40). Whatever else that line’s tradition may hold about its purpose, the
declaration on which it would rest concedes in its own words that the purpose does not reach everyone. A reader who
was taught that his kind is uniformly bound to that work is not reading the founding text of the claim, which says
otherwise in the sentence that makes it. Nothing here claims that the concealed ones are uniformly or generally so
committed; 72:11 and 72:14 are their own testimony against that reading.

What the structure says about this case
The child receives the arrangement at level one and owes nothing for having received it. What was done to his innate
disposition (fitrah) was done to him, and the report on innate disposition names the parents as the agents.
Once capacity and a conclusive proof have both arrived, continuation is his. When he transmits the arrangement
onward, the weight of the section on level three attaches, and compounds with the number who follow.
The remedy of the section on turning back remains available throughout, and it is worth restating here what it does not
require: he need not reconstruct how the arrangement entered his family, nor determine which ancestor first sought it,
nor establish what any of them received. The fourth condition binds him to what he passed on, not to what he was
handed.

Where the chain begins, and why it cannot be read
from inside
The regress terminates
If each generation is formed by the last, where did the corruption enter?
The tradition terminates the regress at both ends, ontologically.
The first man was taught directly: “and He taught Adam the names, all of them” (wa ’allama Adama al-asma’a
kullaha, 2:31). And when he erred, the remedy was itself given to him rather than discovered by him: “then Adam
received words from his Lord, and He accepted his repentance” (fa talaqqa Adamu min rabbihi kalimatin fa taba
’alayh, 2:37).
The refusal on the other side is a dated event with a stated cause: “I am better than him; You created me from fire and
created him from clay” (ana khayrun minhu khalaqtani min narin wa khalaqtahu min tin, 7:12).
Neither species begins corrupt. The chain is finite, and it is finite in both directions.

And it is nonetheless unreadable
But none of that is reachable from inside a formation several generations deep. The person cannot trace himself to the
origin. He has no record. The people who could have told him were themselves told by others.
The chain is finite in fact and inaccessible in practice, and the gap between those two facts is the problem the tradition
actually addresses. It does not address it by supplying the missing record. It addresses it by making the outcome not
depend on having one.
A note for the section on turning back: the first error in the record is answered not by reconstructing the fall but by
words taught for turning back. The remedy precedes the audit and does not require it.

The tradition does not resolve by lineage
Resolution comes instead by the availability of a rebuke, and the Qur’an makes the point by refusing determinism in
both directions.
Abraham needed no genealogy. He needed an object his father had carved and a question about it: “Why do you
worship what does not hear, does not see, and avails you nothing?” (lima ta’budu ma la yasma’u wa la yubsiru wa la
yughni ’anka shay’a, 19:42). The rebuke was available in the room. It required no access to the chain and no
reconstruction of how the household arrived at its practice.
Moses was formed inside the tyrant’s own household, and the forming is attributed elsewhere: “that you might be
formed under My eye” (wa li-tusna’a ’ala ’ayni, 20:39). The verb is one of fashioning or making. He was formed in
that house and formed for something else.
The wife of Pharaoh prayed for a house near God (66:11) while married to the man claiming divinity. Proximity to
corruption at its maximum did not determine her.
And the inheritance breaks downward as readily as upward. Of Noah’s son: “he is not of your family; he is an act
other than righteous” (innahu laysa min ahlik innahu ’amalun ghayru salih, 11:46). A righteous father, a lost son, and
the disqualification stated in terms of deed rather than descent.

The pattern is consistent. Corrupt household, chosen occupant. Righteous house, lost child. Lineage predicts nothing,
in either direction, and the operative variable is whether a rebuke was available and what was done with it.

The guard: guidance is not self-verifiable
The section on lineage creates a hazard that must be closed explicitly, because a reader will otherwise close it the
wrong way. If formation does not determine outcome, and some are guided out of corrupt formations while others are
not, a reader in the first category will reach for the inference that his own divergence verifies his election. That
inference is available, it is flattering, and the tradition forecloses it from both sides.
“You do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills” (innaka la tahdi man ahbabta wa lakinna Allaha
yahdi man yasha’, 28:56). Guidance is not in the gift even of the one to whom the verse is addressed.
“So do not claim purity for yourselves; He knows best who is mindful of Him” (fa la tuzakku anfusakum, huwa
a’lamu bi-mani ittaqa, 53:32). The prohibition is on the self-assessment specifically.
And the petition “Guide us to the straight path” (ihdina as-sirat al-mustaqim, 1:6) is in the present tense, asked five
times daily, by people who already believe. If guidance were a status one could confirm in oneself, the petition would
be redundant for exactly the people who are commanded to make it.
The narrower claim, which is the one this paper makes. Classical commentary explains the daily petition as a request
for steadfastness, continuity, and increase in guidance rather than as evidence that a believer knows nothing of his own
state. What the sources support is the prohibition of final self-certification: a person may not certify himself as finally
pure, elect, or secure from deviation. Guidance (hidayah) does not become a receipt, and the petition is asked by those
who already believe precisely because the thing asked for is continuance.

Turning back
Everything to this point describes a situation. None of it tells the person inside one what to do.

The remedy does not require reading the chain
Turning back (tawbah) is indexed to the person’s present position, not to his causal history. It does not require him to
know how he arrived, to reconstruct his formation, or to apportion blame among those who formed him.
This property is not incidental. the section on level three established that the chain is finite and unreadable from
inside. If the remedy required reading it, there would be no remedy available in exactly the cases that most need one.
The prescription is available precisely where the diagnosis says the record fails. The pattern is set at the first error in
the record. Adam is not asked to reconstruct the fall. He is taught words, and he turns.

The address is to the transgressor
“Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of God; indeed God
forgives all sins” (qul ya ’ibadiya alladhina asrafu ’ala anfusihim la taqnatu min rahmati Allah, inna Allaha yaghfiru
adh-dhunuba jami’an, 39:53).

The address is to those already deep in it, and what it forecloses is despair specifically. This is worth noticing, because
despair is precisely the response that unreadability of one’s own formation tends to produce. A person who cannot
account for how he was made can conclude that the making is permanent. The verse is placed against that conclusion.

The conditions, and the one that bears on transmission
Classical treatment requires desisting from the act (iqla’), regret (nadam), and resolve not to return (’azm ’ala ’adam
al-’awd). Where the wrong touched the rights of others, a fourth condition binds: restoration of what was wrongfully
taken (radd al-mazalim).
The fourth condition as classically formulated concerns identifiable interpersonal rights: wealth wrongfully taken,
bodily harm, injury to honour, and their like.
As a proposed ethical implication: a person who transmitted a formation has not completed his turning by internal
change alone, because what he transmitted continues operating in others after he has stopped, and the weight attached
to that continuation. This is offered as an implication of the structure, not as a binding condition of valid repentance,
and no reader should conclude that his own turning is invalid on its strength.
What would ground it, if anything does: juristic treatments of erroneous teaching and of public misguidance.

The compounding runs both ways
The same mechanism that makes transmission the heaviest thing a person does makes correction the most
effective. Whoever institutes a good practice has its reward and the reward of all who act upon it after him. A
corrected transmission compounds exactly as a corrupt one did.
The exchange is stated more strongly still: for those who turn, believe, and act rightly, “God will replace their evil
deeds with good” (fa ula’ika yubaddilu Allahu sayyi’atihim hasanat, 25:70). The record is not merely cleared. It is
converted.

Not a state but a repeated act
“Indeed God loves those who turn back constantly” (inna Allaha yuhibbu at-tawwabin, 2:222). The form is intensive:
those who turn much, repeatedly, not those who turned once.
And the completion at 20:82 is not “repented” but repented, believed, acted rightly, and then remained
guided (thumma ihtada). The final clause is a continuation, not a certificate.
Turning is a channel held open rather than a status achieved. This is the same guard as the section on the guard,
applied to the remedy: a person who treats his correction as a completed credential has stopped doing the thing the
credential was supposed to name.

The door is open, and it is not open indefinitely
Repentance is accepted, in the prophetic report, as long as the death rattle has not reached the throat, and 4:17-18
excludes those who persist until death arrives.
Both halves matter. The availability is real, and it is bounded. A structure that reported only the first would be
sentimental; one that reported only the second would be despairing, and 39:53 forbids that.

Addressed to the one inside
The preceding sections describe a structure. This one states what it means for a person actually occupying it, because
a reader in that position deserves the conclusion rather than an inference he must assemble himself.

On who this is for, and why it is not stated as a label
This section is for anyone formed inside an arrangement he did not choose: those who received it and have not yet
weighed it, those who have continued it, and those who have passed it on.
It is deliberately not addressed to a category of person. The structure developed above forbids the label, and the
forbidding is not delicacy. A person who received an arrangement at level one is, by the tradition’s own accounting,
carrying nothing. To address him as though he were already among the corrupters would contradict the section on
level one in the act of citing it. And the corrupters described at 6:112 are those who are actively at work, which is a
description of conduct at level three rather than of anyone’s origin.
The relevant question is never what a person is. It is where he stands and which way he is facing.

The direction of transmission, stated precisely
Nothing flows backward onto a descendant. “No bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another” (53:38) is stated
without qualification and repeated across the Qur’an, and it settles the matter that most often troubles a person in this
position: he does not carry his ancestors’ account. Not diminished, not partially. He does not carry it.
What moves forward is the transmitter’s own act. The instituted-practice report measures that act by its extent, so a
person who passed something on answers for the passing. He does not answer for having received.
This asymmetry is the whole of the good news and the whole of the demand. The past is not chargeable to him. The
future is.

If you were placed there
You owe nothing for the placement. The pen does not record against a child, and what was done to your innate
disposition was done to you: the report on innate disposition names the parents as the agents and compares the result
to an injury, not to a choice.
Whatever was arranged before you could speak is not on your account. There is no entry to answer for and no
inherited stain to work off.

If you have continued it
The tradition anticipates the answer you will reach for, and records it in the voice of the person who gives it: “We
found our fathers upon a way, and we are following in their footsteps” (43:22). It appears at 2:170, 5:104, 7:28, and
31:21, and 43:23 notes that every warner before was met with the same reply.
It is recorded in order to be refused. the section on lineage shows why: the Qur’an resolves these cases by what was
available to the person, not by what his family did, and it breaks inheritance in both directions, so that no household is
a sentence and none is a guarantee.
But notice what the refusal implies. If lineage does not condemn you, it also cannot excuse you, and those are the
same fact. The reason your fathers cannot damn you is the reason they cannot answer for you. The structure that lifts
the weight of your origin is the structure that hands you the weight of your continuation.

If you passed it on
This is where the weight is heaviest, and it is also where the strongest thing in the tradition applies to you specifically.
The report on instituted practice is one sentence with two halves. The half usually quoted says that whoever institutes
an evil practice bears its burden and the burden of all who act on it after him. The other half says that whoever
institutes a good practice has its reward and the reward of all who act on it after him.
The mechanism is the same in both directions. If you transmitted something and it compounded against you, then a
corrected transmission compounds for you by the identical arithmetic. The person best positioned to institute a
correction is the person who instituted the thing being corrected, because he is already the origin of a chain and
already known to those standing in it.
This is not consolation offered in place of the reckoning. It is the reckoning’s own structure read in the other direction.

On whether you are past the point
Someone who has gone far into such an arrangement tends to conclude that a line was crossed behind him. That
conclusion has a specific answer in the text, and the answer sits in an unusual place.
At 25:68 the Qur’an names the gravest acts: setting up another alongside God, killing the soul that God forbade, and
unlawful intercourse. At 25:69 it says the punishment will be multiplied and that he will abide in it disgraced. That is
the eternal-abiding language, stated at maximum severity.
And 25:70 begins: “except whoever turns back, and believes, and acts rightly” (illa man taba wa amana wa ’amila
’amalan salihan). Of those, “God will replace their evil deeds with good” (fa ula’ika yubaddilu Allahu sayyi’atihim
hasanat).
The threat and its exemption are adjacent verses. The tradition raises the possibility of permanent loss and forecloses
it in the next breath, for the gravest acts it has just finished naming. And the exchange is not erasure. The record is
converted.
The general statement is broader still: “O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the
mercy of God; indeed God forgives all sins” (39:53). The address is to transgressors, and what it forbids by name is
despair.
The Qur’an states that associating partners with God is not forgiven while lesser things are forgiven to whom He wills
(4:48, 4:116). The commentary holds these verses to concern one who meets God in that state, and repentance from it
before death is accepted by consensus, consistent with 25:70 and 39:53.

What is actually asked
Four things, and none of them is that you understand your own history.
Desist. Stop. This is first in the classical ordering and it is not softened by anything above.
Regret, and resolve not to return. Interior, and neither is a feeling you must manufacture on demand; both are
positions you take.
Reach what you passed on. Where the wrong touched others, restoration binds (the subsection on the conditions of
turning back). For a transmitter this is the operative condition, because what he transmitted is still running in people
after he has stopped. What that requires in a given case is not something this paper can specify, and whether the
condition is correctly applied to a transmitter is item four of the review request.

And keep turning. The form at 2:222 is intensive, and the completion at 20:82 is “and then remained guided.” Not a
certificate. A direction held.

Conclusion
The tradition’s treatment of the formed agent separates three things that ordinary moral language runs together. Where
a person was placed carries nothing. What he continues, once proof has reached him, carries something. What he
passes on carries most, and carries more the further it travels. It refuses to resolve cases by lineage, so that no
household is a sentence and no lineage a guarantee.

Appendix B. A superseded document by the same
author, recorded
In July 2026 this author wrote a document titled The Architecture of Demonic Deception in Islamic Theology. Parts of
it are absorbed above: its treatment of the assigned companion (qarin), of the limits on satanic power, and of divine
justice for the unreached. Other parts are withdrawn.
What was withdrawn. The document claimed that most of humanity is subject to a multi-generational deception
involving lifelong induced states from infancy and a fabricated history, that a divinely protected minority is persecuted
by the deluded majority, and that this persecution validates the protected.
Why.
It contradicted itself. A deception imposed on infants from birth is precisely the involuntary subjugation that the
document’s own opening section declared incompatible with divine justice (al-’adl). Its later sections falsified its
earlier ones.
And it had no remedy. Its closing address told a suffering reader that his suffering was validation. It did not tell him
what to do. The section on turning back above is the answer that document was reaching for and did not find, and it
differs in the decisive respect: it is addressed to the transgressor rather than the vindicated, and it prescribes an act
rather than conferring a status.
