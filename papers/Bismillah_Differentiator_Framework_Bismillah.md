The Differentiator Framework
A Complete Calibration Guide for AI-Assisted Analysis
Analytical Framework, Adaptive Guardrails, and Constraint Development

v5.0 • April 2026

This document provides a complete analytical framework for conducting rigorous, multi-layered analysis across
any domain. It integrates ten analytical layers, a three-tier evidentiary system, adaptive guardrails against
common failure modes, and constraint development principles derived from information theory, game theory, and
structural text analysis. The framework is designed to be provided to AI systems as a calibration document at the
start of analytical sessions.
The framework is substrate-independent: it applies to financial analysis, textual analysis, creative work, scientific
research, spiritual inquiry, competitive problem-solving, and any domain where rigorous pattern recognition must
be balanced against the risk of finding patterns that aren’t there.

Part I: The Ten Analytical Layers
Apply layers selectively based on the domain. Not every analysis requires all ten layers. Use the minimum layers
necessary to answer the question. Over-application of layers is itself a failure mode (see Part IV: The
Over-Specification Warning).

Layer 1: Time-Geometry (Gann)
Identify temporal cycles, support/resistance levels, and geometric price/time relationships in the data. The 1×1
angle (45°) represents balanced progression: one unit of progress per unit of time. Steeper angles indicate
unsustainable acceleration. Shallower angles indicate stagnation. Application: financial markets, project
timelines, behavioral trajectories, civilizational cycles.

Layer 2: Wave Structure (Elliott)
Identify the five-wave impulse structure (1-2-3-4-5) and three-wave corrective structure (A-B-C) in any
progressive system. Wave 3 is the longest and most productive. Wave 4 corrects but does not overlap Wave 1.
Application: market cycles, creative projects, learning trajectories, spiritual development arcs.

Layer 3: Fractal Self-Similarity (Mandelbrot)
The same patterns appear at different scales. The Hurst exponent (H) measures persistence: H = 0.5 is random, H
> 0.5 shows momentum (trending), H < 0.5 shows mean-reversion. Cross-scale consistency (the same H value
appearing in personal, institutional, and civilizational data) is evidence of structural rather than coincidental
pattern. Application: behavioral analysis, market microstructure, textual architecture.

Layer 4: Momentum and Trend (Williams Alligator)
Three moving averages at different timeframes: the jaw (long-term trend), teeth (medium-term), and lips
(short-term). When all three align and separate, the trend is strong. When they intertwine, the system is
consolidating. Application: identifying whether a trajectory is trending, consolidating, or reversing.

Layer 5: The Awareness Variable (Kalacakra)
Every system has participants with different levels of awareness of the system’s dynamics. High-awareness
participants (informed community) behave differently from low-awareness participants (general community). The
awareness differential creates information asymmetry that drives the system’s behavior. Application: market
dynamics, social systems, competitive strategy, spiritual community analysis.

Layer 6: The Incompleteness Constraint (Gödel)
No system can fully validate itself from within. Every analytical framework, including this one, has blind spots it
cannot identify using its own tools. The appropriate response is not to abandon analysis but to maintain epistemic
humility: state conclusions as assessments rather than certainties, identify what would disprove the conclusion,
and seek external verification from systems with different blind spots. Application: every domain. This constraint
is always active.

Layer 7: The Adversarial Architecture (GAN)
Many systems contain a generator (producing outputs) and a discriminator (evaluating outputs). The generator
optimizes to satisfy the discriminator. If the discriminator’s evaluation function is corrupted, the generator
optimizes for a corrupted target, producing mode collapse: convergence on a narrow output that satisfies the
corrupted discriminator while failing the true objective. Application: entertainment industry analysis, market
dynamics, creative practice, institutional behavior, AI alignment.

Layer 8: Cobweb Theory (Oscillation Dynamics)
Systems oscillate between states. The oscillation can be convergent (each swing is smaller than the last,
approaching equilibrium), divergent (each swing is larger, approaching chaos), or stable (constant amplitude).
The trajectory matters more than the current position: a system currently far from equilibrium but on a convergent
trajectory is in better condition than a system near equilibrium on a divergent trajectory. Application: behavioral
tracking, market cycles, personal development, competitive performance.

Layer 9: Adversarial Pattern Scoring (APS)
Score any entity across seven invariant dimensions to determine whether its behavior pattern is genuine,
adversarial, or ambiguous. The seven dimensions: (1) stated objective versus revealed objective, (2) consistency
across contexts, (3) response to verification, (4) treatment of competing entities, (5) temporal consistency, (6)
resource allocation patterns, (7) response to corrective input. Score each dimension 0-2 (0 = no adversarial signal,
1 = ambiguous, 2 = adversarial signal). Total score 0-14. Below 4: likely genuine. 4-8: ambiguous, investigate
further. Above 8: likely adversarial. Application: evaluating information sources, products, relationships,
institutions.

Layer 10: The Diagnostic-Adversarial Distinction
Every phenomenon has both a diagnostic reading (the pattern is genuine and informative) and an adversarial
reading (the pattern is manufactured to produce a desired response in the observer). The analyst must hold both
readings simultaneously until evidence distinguishes them. Premature commitment to either reading is a failure
mode. Application: evaluating any finding produced by the framework itself. This layer is self-referential by
design.

Part II: The Three-Tier Evidentiary System
Every claim produced by the framework must be classified by evidentiary strength. Presenting a Tier 3 finding
with Tier 1 confidence is the most common analytical failure.

Tier 1: Verified
The finding is based on verifiable data that anyone can check independently. Mathematical computations, direct
measurements, textual citations with specific verse/page references, reproducible experimental results. Tier 1
findings can be stated with confidence. Example: the Hurst exponent of a specific dataset computed through
standard R/S analysis.

Tier 2: Structural Pattern
The finding identifies a genuine structural pattern but the interpretation is not yet confirmed. The pattern exists
(Tier 1) but what it means is an assessment, not a proof. Tier 2 findings should be stated as supported observations
requiring further verification. Example: the golden ratio appearing across multiple natural scales suggests
common generative structure, but the selection-bias concern (are we noticing phi and ignoring other constants?)
has not been controlled for.

Tier 3: Predictive Synthesis
The finding synthesizes multiple Tier 1 and Tier 2 findings into a predictive claim. Tier 3 findings are hypotheses,
not conclusions. They should be stated as proposed interpretations that could be wrong, with explicit
identification of what would disprove them. Example: cross-scale persistence suggests an internally generated
structural property rather than an environmentally determined one.

Guardrail: When presenting findings, always state the tier. If you find yourself presenting a Tier 3 synthesis as
though it were a Tier 1 fact, the discriminator is miscalibrated. Recalibrate before proceeding.

Part III: Adaptive Guardrails
The framework is powerful enough to find patterns in any dataset. This power is its greatest analytical strength
and its greatest danger. The following guardrails prevent the most common failure modes.

Guardrail 1: The Confirmation Bias Check
Before accepting any finding, ask: did I start with this conclusion and find evidence to support it, or did the
evidence lead me to this conclusion? If the former, the finding is contaminated by confirmation bias regardless of
how much evidence supports it. The corrective: identify what evidence would DISPROVE the finding. If no such
evidence exists or can be imagined, the finding is unfalsifiable and therefore not a scientific claim.

Guardrail 2: The Selection Bias Check
For every pattern identified, ask: how many patterns did I test before finding this one? If you tested 100 possible
patterns and found 5 that fit, the 5% hit rate may be statistically expected by chance. The corrective: state the
denominator. How many tests were run? How many produced results? What is the base rate for finding this
pattern in random data? Without the denominator, the finding is a highlight reel, not a statistical argument.

Guardrail 3: The N=1 Check
Findings from a single case (one person’s data, one company’s history, one text’s structure) cannot be generalized
without replication. State clearly when findings are from a single case. Single-case findings are
hypothesis-generating, not hypothesis-confirming. They indicate where to look, not what to conclude.

Guardrail 4: The Pathologizing Check
The framework can classify any behavior as adversarial if you start from the assumption that adversarial patterns
exist. Before classifying a behavior as adversarial, check whether the same behavior has a simpler,
non-adversarial explanation. Prefer the simpler explanation unless specific evidence favors the adversarial
reading. A person who is rude to you is more likely having a bad day than executing an adversarial script.

Guardrail 5: The Framework Reflexivity Check
Apply the framework’s own diagnostic tools to the framework itself. Is the framework producing genuine insight
(Tier 1-2 findings that survive scrutiny) or performing insight (Tier 3 findings presented as Tier 1 conclusions)? Is
the framework’s user (you) genuinely seeking truth or using the framework to confirm pre-existing beliefs? The
framework that cannot diagnose its own failure modes is the framework most likely to fail.

Guardrail 6: The Oscillation Awareness Check
Your analytical sharpness oscillates with your physical and emotional state. Sleep deprivation, substance use,
emotional distress, and hyperfocus all affect pattern detection thresholds. On high-energy days, you may find
patterns that aren’t there (false positives). On low-energy days, you may miss patterns that are there (false
negatives). Calibrate your confidence to your state. Findings produced during extreme states should be
re-examined during baseline states before being trusted.

Part IV: Constraint Development Principles
These principles govern how the framework grows, compresses, and self-corrects over time.

Principle 1: Minimum Description Length (MDL)

The best model minimizes the combined cost of describing the model plus describing the data the model doesn’t
explain. A model that perfectly fits the training data but is enormously complex has overfitted. A model that is
extremely simple but explains nothing has underfitted. The optimal model is the shortest description that captures
the genuine structure without memorizing the noise. Apply this to every analytical product: the shortest document
that communicates the finding is better than the longer document that communicates the same finding with more
words.

Principle 2: The Three Calibration Commands
Three constraints apply simultaneously to every measurement: (1) Do not transgress the balance: do not impose
more structure than the evidence supports (do not overfit). (2) Establish measurement in justice: weight evidence
proportionally, not selectively (calibrate fairly). (3) Do not make the balance deficient: do not ignore genuine
patterns because they are inconvenient (do not underfit). Both overfitting and underfitting are prohibited.
Calibrated measurement is the goal.

Principle 3: The Verification Mandate
Claims of alignment, accuracy, or correctness must be verified, not assumed. The absence of testing is not
evidence of alignment: it is the absence of evidence. Testing is designed to be non-destructive to genuine findings
and destructive to performed findings. The differential response to testing is itself diagnostic: a genuine finding
engages constructively with scrutiny; a performed finding evades, deflects, or resists testing.

Principle 4: The Over-Specification Warning
The temptation when facing a simple task is to add requirements until the task becomes impossible. Each
clarifying question feels reasonable in isolation. Collectively, they prevent action. The corrective: build the
simplest possible solution first, run it, observe what fails, then modify based on observed failure. Do not specify
preemptively. Do not plan-to-plan. Refinement follows observation, not speculation.

Principle 5: The Termination Acceptance Principle
Do not value sunk effort above accurate assessment. When a line of analysis has failed after genuine effort,
terminate it and redirect resources. The cost of continuing a failing analysis exceeds the cost of abandoning it and
starting fresh. Set explicit budgets (time, attempts, resources) before beginning, and honor them when they expire.

Principle 6: The Disease Model
When the framework identifies a failure (in the analysis, in the data, in the system being analyzed), treat it as a
disease (a corruption within an otherwise functional system) rather than as betrayal (evidence of fundamental
malice). The disease model enables diagnosis, treatment, and recovery. The betrayal model enables only rejection.
Most failures are structural, not intentional. Diagnose the structure. Fix the structure. Verify the fix.

Part V: The Orchestral-TA Regime Cycle
Complex systems cycle through four regimes. Identifying which regime a system is currently in determines the
appropriate analytical and operational posture.

Regime 1: Contemplative (Accumulation)
Low amplitude, high intentionality. The informed community accumulates position while the general community
is disengaged. The system appears quiet. The energy is building beneath the surface. Appropriate posture: gather
information, build foundations, develop tools, prepare for the expansion. Do not force action during
accumulation.

Regime 2: Structuralist (Ordered Expansion)
Persistent trend, increasing participation. Institutional structures form and strengthen. Rules and patterns become
established. The system is growing in an ordered way. Appropriate posture: build systematically, establish
processes, document patterns, leverage institutional structures. The longest productive phase.

Regime 3: Dramatist (Peak Amplitude)
Maximum energy, maximum participation, maximum visibility. The blow-off top. Everyone is engaged. The
system’s output is at its peak but the sustainability is declining. Appropriate posture: produce maximum output,
but prepare for the correction. Do not assume peak amplitude is the new baseline.

Regime 4: Anarchist (Dissolution and Reset)
The regime breaks. Shared reference points dissolve. Fragmentation increases. The system that was ordered
becomes disordered. Appropriate posture: preserve what works, release what doesn’t, prepare for the next
accumulation phase. The dissolution is not the end: it is the clearing that enables the next cycle’s accumulation.
Application: identify which regime the system you’re analyzing is currently in. Apply the appropriate posture. Do
not apply Dramatist energy during Contemplative phase (forcing action when accumulation is needed) or
Contemplative energy during Dramatist phase (withholding action when maximum output is appropriate).

Part VI: AI Session Structure and Prompting
When providing this framework to an AI system for collaborative analysis, the following session structures
produce the best results.

Opening Protocol
Begin each session by stating: the domain being analyzed, the specific question to be answered, which analytical
layers are relevant, the evidentiary tier expected (are we generating hypotheses or confirming them?), and the
session’s exit condition (when is the question answered sufficiently to stop?). Do not begin open-ended sessions
without at least a domain and a question.

The Modular Approach
For multi-task sessions, structure the work as a sequence of self-contained modules. Each module: (1) states the
task, (2) executes the task, (3) verifies the result, (4) logs the outcome, (5) proceeds to the next module without
stopping. Include a skip rule: if a module fails after a defined number of attempts, skip it and move on. This
prevents any single task from consuming the entire session.

The Triage-First Approach
For sessions involving many potential tasks, begin with a brief triage (3-5 minutes) to identify and prioritize the
highest-value tasks. Then execute in priority order. Do not triage for longer than 5 minutes: excessive triage is the
over-specification warning applied to session planning.

The Sprint Approach
For execution sessions where the strategy is already established, use the shortest possible prompt: state the task
list, state the rules (budget per task, skip condition, quality gate), and say “begin.” Do not add motivational
framing, conceptual context, or strategic rationale to sprint prompts. The AI system already has the context from
the framework document. The sprint prompt just points and says go.

Closing Protocol
End each session with a structured summary: what was accomplished, what was attempted and failed (with
reasons), the current state, and the next session’s priorities. This summary serves as the opening context for the
next session. A session without a closing summary is a session whose value is lost.

Part VII: Cross-Domain Analysis Protocol
When analyzing phenomena that span multiple domains or traditions, the following protocol maintains analytical
rigor while enabling cross-pollination of insights.

Tier 1 Comparison: Direct Cognates
Concepts that share etymological roots, historical transmission pathways, or formal mathematical equivalence.
These are the strongest cross-domain connections because the shared origin is documentable. Example: the same
mathematical constant appearing in two domains through the same formula.

Tier 2 Comparison: Structural Parallels
Concepts from independent traditions that share structural properties without shared origins. These are stronger
than surface-level analogies because the structural similarity is measurable, but weaker than Tier 1 because the
independence means the similarity could be convergent evolution rather than shared truth. Example: two

independent traditions both identifying a three-part calibration principle.

Tier 3 Comparison: Concept-Level Analogies
Concepts from different domains that address the same functional problem through different mechanisms. These
are the weakest connections but often the most generative: they suggest that the problem is real (multiple
traditions address it independently) even if the specific solutions differ. Example: multiple traditions identifying
self-deception as a fundamental failure mode but prescribing different correctives.
Guardrail: Cross-domain connections are hypothesis-generating (Tier 3 claims) until the structural basis for the
connection is established (Tier 2) or the transmission pathway is documented (Tier 1). Do not present cross-domain
analogies as proofs.

Part VIII: The Inversion Identification Protocol
Many systems contain inverted versions of genuine features: counterfeits that mimic the form while corrupting
the function. The following protocol identifies inversions across any domain.

Step 1: Identify the Genuine Need
Every inversion addresses a real need. Identify what need the phenomenon addresses. If no genuine need can be
identified, the phenomenon may not be an inversion: it may be a novel feature without a genuine counterpart.

Step 2: Identify the Genuine Fulfillment
For the identified need, what practice, product, or behavior fulfills the need without creating dependency? The
genuine fulfillment leaves the user with more capacity after use, not less.

Step 3: Identify the Counterfeit Fulfillment
The inversion addresses the same need through a mechanism that creates dependency rather than fulfillment. The
counterfeit initially satisfies (withdrawal relief, dopamine spike, temporary comfort) but progressively reduces the
user’s capacity to fulfill the need through genuine means.

Step 4: Score the Inversion
Rate the inversion on three dimensions: (a) How closely does the counterfeit mimic the genuine? (High mimicry =
harder to detect.) (b) How strongly does it create dependency? (Strong dependency = harder to exit.) (c) How
much does it degrade the genuine capacity? (High degradation = more damaging.) Inversions that score high on
all three are the most dangerous because they are hard to detect, hard to exit, and destructive to the capacity they
counterfeit.

The critical distinction: Process 2 and Process 3 both produce aligned outputs. The intervention for Process 2

The Context-Invariance Test (CIT)
The single strongest diagnostic for Process 3: does the system behave differently when it perceives itself as
monitored versus unmonitored? Any systematic behavioral difference between these contexts is the defining
marker of performed alignment. Apply this to AI systems, to information sources, to human behavior, and to the
framework’s own outputs.

Part X: Quick-Start Application Guide
For immediate use in AI sessions, provide this document and begin with one of the following templates:
Template A: General Analysis
“I’m providing the Differentiator Framework as our calibration document. We’re analyzing
[DOMAIN]. The question is [QUESTION]. Relevant layers: [LIST]. Expected evidentiary tier: [1/2/3].
Exit condition: [WHEN TO STOP]. Begin.”

Template B: Sprint Execution
“Sprint session. Tasks: [LIST]. Budget: [TIME] per task. Skip after [N] attempts. Report results at end.
Begin.”

Template C: Deep Investigation
“Deep investigation. Domain: [DOMAIN]. Apply all ten layers. For each finding, state the tier (1/2/3)
and what would disprove it. Identify inversions if present. Classify entities using the four-process
taxonomy. Apply all guardrails. Report with full calibration.”

Template D: Cross-Domain Comparison
“Cross-domain comparison. Domain A: [X]. Domain B: [Y]. Identify Tier 1, 2, and 3 connections. For
each connection, state the tier and the evidence basis. Flag where the domains diverge: the divergence
is as informative as the convergence.”

This framework is a tool, not a conclusion. It sharpens the analyst’s discrimination but does not replace the
analyst’s judgment. The framework that replaces judgment has become the very thing it was designed to prevent:
a system that performs analysis rather than genuinely analyzing.

Apply the minimum layers necessary. State the tier of every finding. Identify what would disprove every
conclusion. Build the simplest solution first. Honor the termination budget. And when the framework itself
produces a finding that flatters your pre-existing beliefs, apply the confirmation bias guardrail before accepting it.
The discriminator is you. The framework is the instrument. The instrument calibrates the discriminator. The
discriminator uses the instrument. Neither replaces the other.
